DriftGate changes only how a request is answered. It keeps the trained device and edge models as they are and adds three steps that follow from Section~\ref{sec:motivation}. It combines the two exits instead of choosing one, it corrects the edge exit for the class mix of its cell, and it sets the weight of each exit without labels. A fourth step limits offloading to the requests where the edge is most useful. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} shows the resulting path of a request.

\subsection{Combining the Two Exits}
\label{sec:combine}

Section~\ref{sec:complementary} showed that the device exit is accurate on Main requests and the edge exit on OOP and OOR requests, and Section~\ref{sec:choose} showed that the device exit cannot tell these requests apart by its own confidence. DriftGate therefore uses both exits on every offloaded request and answers with
\begin{equation}
\hat y(x)=\arg\max_c\; w_k\,\pd(c\,|\,x)+(1-w_k)\,\pec(c\,|\,x),
\label{eq:combine}
\end{equation}
where $\pec$ is the corrected edge output of Section~\ref{sec:correct} and $w_k\in[0,1]$ is the weight of the device exit.

The form of the combination matters. On a Main request, the device exit usually puts a large probability on the correct class, and the average follows it unless the edge exit puts a larger weighted probability on another class. On an OOP request, the device exit has never learned the correct class and spreads its probability over its own classes, while the edge exit may put a large probability on the correct class. The average keeps $(1-w_k)$ times this probability, so the edge answer wins when this term exceeds the combined probability of every other class, which happens when the edge exit is confident and the device exit spreads its probability. A product of probabilities, which is what adding logits computes~\cite{chen2022fedrod,wolczyk2021ztw}, scales the edge probability of the correct class by the device probability of that class. When the device probability is close to zero, the product can overrule a confident edge answer. The literature on combining probability forecasts describes the same contrast between linear and logarithmic pooling~\cite{genest1986combining}. Which combination is more accurate depends on the setting in our evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Correcting the Edge Exit for Its Cell}
\label{sec:correct}

The average in Eq.~\eqref{eq:combine} also depends on how the edge exit weighs the device's own classes, and by default the edge exit discounts them. A classifier trained on data with class mix $\pi$ produces probabilities proportional to $p(x\,|\,c)\,\pi(c)$. The edge exit is trained on the class mix $\pi_k$ of the cell (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pi}), in which the device's own classes have a share $a_k$ of about 0.25 in the commute scenario. A device at home, however, receives own-class requests about 88\% of the time. The edge exit therefore discounts exactly the classes that dominate the device's traffic, and on a Main request it often prefers a class of another device in the cell.

DriftGate reduces this discount with a rescaling that follows Bayes' rule~\cite{saerens2002adjusting}. If the class-conditional distributions were the same in training and at inference and the edge probabilities were calibrated, replacing the training mix in this way would give the posterior under the new mix. Neither assumption is guaranteed for the edge exit, so we treat the rescaling as a heuristic and evaluate it empirically. It replaces the training mix $\pi_k$ with a target mix that keeps the relative frequencies within the device's own classes and within the other classes, but gives the other classes a total share $r$:
\begin{equation}
\pec(c\,|\,x)\propto
\begin{cases}
\pe(c\,|\,x)\,\dfrac{1-r}{a_k}, & c\in M_k,\\[2mm]
\pe(c\,|\,x)\,\dfrac{r}{1-a_k}, & c\notin M_k,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:correct}
\end{equation}
normalized to sum to one. We set $r=1/2$, so the corrected edge exit treats the device's own classes and all other classes as equally likely before seeing the request.

We use this even split instead of an estimate of the device's current mix for three reasons. First, the true share changes with the device's location, from about 0.115 at home to about 0.51 away in the commute scenario, and the edge cannot observe it. Second, an estimate computed from the edge exit inherits the bias that the correction is meant to remove. In our development runs (three seeds that we do not use for evaluation, rounds 30 and later), the EM estimate of Saerens \emph{et al.}~\cite{saerens2002adjusting} gave 0.23 for devices away from home, where the true share was 0.51. Third, the combined answer does not need the edge exit to know the device's preference for its own classes, because the device exit already carries that preference. The edge exit only needs to stop discounting those classes. The correction uses only $M_k$ and $\pi_k$, which the edge already holds from training.

\subsection{Setting the Weight without Labels}
\label{sec:weight}

The weight $w_k$ decides how much the device exit counts in Eq.~\eqref{eq:combine}. Its best value depends on the model and the split point. With a small device block, the edge exit is the stronger classifier on most requests. With a large device block, such as the first half of a ResNet-18, the device exit can be stronger than the edge exit overall. A weight tuned for one model can be far from the best for another (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), and labels are not available to tune it after deployment.

DriftGate sets the weight from how confident the two exits are on the device's recent traffic. Let $\Hd_k$ and $\He_k$ be the mean entropies of the device exit and the edge exit over a window of recent offloaded requests of device $k$, not including the current request. DriftGate uses
\begin{equation}
w_k=\frac{\He_k}{\Hd_k+\He_k},
\label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
so the exit that has been more confident on this device's requests receives the larger weight. Section~\ref{sec:choose} showed that the confidence of the device exit on one request is a weak signal. The mean over many requests answers a different question. It does not try to identify the kind of each request, but measures which exit is generally sharper for this device, model, and split point. In our implementation, the window holds the device's earlier offloaded requests of the current evaluation period once there are at least eight of them, and otherwise the offloaded requests of the device's previous period. Before any such requests exist, DriftGate uses $w_k=1/2$. In the commute scenario, $w_k$ is 0.42 on average with a standard deviation of 0.04 within a run, and with ResNet-18 it is 0.59. The weight thus settles near a value set by the model and split point more than it follows individual requests.

\subsection{Offloading Only Uncertain Requests}
\label{sec:offload}

Combining requires the edge output, so every request that DriftGate combines must be offloaded. When the network or the edge is a bottleneck, the device can answer some requests alone. DriftGate keeps the confidence of the device exit for this purpose. A request with $H(\pd(\cdot\,|\,x))\le\tau$ is answered with the device exit, and the other requests are offloaded and combined. The threshold $\tau$ follows the offloading budget $\beta$, for example as the $(1-\beta)$ quantile of recent device-exit entropies. The confidence of the device exit is too weak to choose the exit that answers a request, but it is useful for ranking which requests gain least from the edge. Requests with very low device-exit entropy are more often Main requests that the device exit already answers correctly, so they gain least from the edge. Since only offloaded requests have an edge output, both $\Hd_k$ and $\He_k$ are computed over the same offloaded requests.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DriftGate inference for a request $x$ of device $k$}
\label{alg:driftgate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE threshold $\tau$, window of entropies of earlier offloaded requests of device $k$; edge holds $M_k$, $\pi_k$, and $r$
\STATE compute $h_k(x)$ and $\pd(\cdot\,|\,x)$ on the device
\IF{$H(\pd(\cdot\,|\,x))\le\tau$}
  \RETURN $\arg\max_c \pd(c\,|\,x)$
\ENDIF
\STATE send $h_k(x)$ to the edge server
\STATE \textit{edge:} compute $\pe(\cdot\,|\,x)$, $H(\pe(\cdot\,|\,x))$, and $\pec(\cdot\,|\,x)$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:correct}; return $\pec$ and the entropy
\STATE $w_k\leftarrow \He_k/(\Hd_k+\He_k)$ from the window, or $1/2$ if no window exists yet
\STATE $\hat y\leftarrow\arg\max_c\; w_k\pd(c\,|\,x)+(1-w_k)\pec(c\,|\,x)$
\STATE add $H(\pd)$ and $H(\pe)$ of this request to the window
\RETURN $\hat y$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Deployment and Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:driftgate} summarizes DriftGate. The edge applies the correction because it already holds $M_k$ and the class counts of its training data. Instead of a single label, it returns $C$ corrected probabilities and one entropy value, an application payload of 44 bytes for ten classes in single precision. For a device in two cells, both edges compute their logits and the average of the two is corrected (Section~\ref{sec:training}), so such a request takes two edge calls; in S1 this adds 0.13 calls per offloaded request. The device keeps two running sums for the weight and computes one weighted average of two $C$-dimensional vectors per offloaded request. With NumPy on one core of a server CPU (AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 7965WX), the correction, the weighted average, and the argmax take 3.2\,$\mu$s per request when 20,000 requests are processed one at a time. Training, the models, and the uplink payload stay unchanged.
